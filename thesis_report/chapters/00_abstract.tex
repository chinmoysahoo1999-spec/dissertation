\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models write fluent prose that is, often enough to matter, confidently
wrong. This failure --- hallucination --- is the main barrier to using these models where
correctness counts. One family of detectors reads a model's own hidden activations as it
writes and learns to separate truthful continuations from fabricated ones, with no external
retrieval and no repeated sampling. Such detectors are cheap to run, yet the published
evidence for them is spread thinly across different models, datasets, and hardware budgets,
which makes it hard to say which ingredient actually carries the signal.

This dissertation settles that question with one reproducible study that runs end to end on
free cloud GPUs. Building on the label-free data generation of the MIND framework --- where a
model continues a truncated encyclopedia article and is labelled by whether it recovers the
original entity --- we design three inexpensive signals read from a single forward pass: how
far the representation travels from one layer to the next, how widely it spreads across the
layer stack, and how uncertain the model is as it writes. Around these we run a systematic
ablation of \textbf{sixteen feature configurations} --- the bare hidden state, each new
signal alone and together, nine features taken from the recent literature, six further
exploratory signals, and their combinations. Every configuration shares one classifier, one
data split, and one set of scoring rows, and each is measured against \textbf{six detectors}
--- four state-of-the-art methods and two simple references --- across as many as ten
question-answering, summarisation, and dialogue benchmarks, on several open models ranging
from one to seven billion parameters.

The study separates two regimes that prior work tends to blur: detecting hallucinations in
text of the same kind the detector was trained on, and transferring to unseen task types.
In-distribution detection is reliable; transfer is the hard part, and there the outcome is
genuinely model-dependent. On one six-billion-parameter model a supervised mid-layer probe
is strongest while our richer configurations sit just behind it; on a second model of the
same scale a configuration built from the exploratory internal-state signals is the
strongest method of all, ahead of every baseline. The detectors also differ sharply in cost:
every single-pass method, ours and most baselines alike, is far cheaper at inference than
the sampling-based detector, which repeats generation several times per input.

The contribution is therefore a careful, free-tier-reproducible map of which internal-state
signals are worth their cost and where they fail --- the controlled, like-for-like comparison
this corner of the field has lacked --- rather than a single headline number. Along the way
the work delivers a reusable pipeline, a clean per-configuration and per-baseline result set
on two open models, and an honest account of the limits of transferring an
encyclopedia-trained detector to other tasks.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hallucination detection, large language models, internal
representations, hidden states, probing classifiers, feature ablation, reproducible
evaluation, inference cost.
